Does an employment gap on your resume hurt?

Short gaps showed no clear cost in the biggest review; long ones cut replies. A reason line helped in one test, not in another. The bias is the employer’s.

By Cesar Enrriquez-Zuniga. Published October 4, 2026. Updated October 7, 2026. How this was made: How we research

What to do

  • Still searching? For the first six months, there is no gap to explain.
  • Out a year or more: list real work, study or volunteering you did.
  • Keep every date true; a one-line reason is your call.

What the evidence says

  • Short gaps: up to 6 months out of work showed no clear cost in a 2026 review of 16 field tests; its authors read a plus (Big study; D’hert et al. 2026).
  • Long gaps: 13 months or more cut employer replies by about a fifth or more in that review; US tests disagree on when it starts (Big studies; D’hert et al. 2026; Kroft et al. 2013; Farber et al. 2016).
  • A reason line: a health reason did better than silence in one US test; a childcare reason changed nothing in a UK test (Big studies; Namingit et al. 2021; Kristal et al. 2023).
  • Never change dates: background checks can compare them (Vendor survey; HireRight 2025a). The bias is the employer’s, not a flaw in you.

Does a gap in your work history cost you interviews?

A long gap often does. A short one showed no clear cost in the biggest review so far.

That review came out in 2026. It combined 16 field experiments with almost 67,000 made-up applicants in seven countries. Each experiment sent employers resumes that differed in whether the applicant was out of work, and for how long. The review compared applicants out of work now with applicants who had a job (D’hert et al. 2026, pp. 1357, 1362). A callback means the employer answers and asks for an interview or more details. Studies call it a callback, a positive reply or an interview request.

Length made the difference. Up to six months out of work, positive replies were about 8% higher than for applicants with a job. That difference could be chance. From 13 to 18 months, replies were about 21% lower. From 19 to 36 months, they were about 27% lower. The 8% figure is not adjusted for differences between studies; the two long-gap figures are. With that adjustment, and the most extreme studies set aside, short spells showed a plus of about 17% (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1382). The review’s summary says the harm becomes noticeable after about twelve months. The review’s text gives no number for 7 to 12 months. Its chart shows replies a little lower for that span, and that difference could be chance (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1381).

Across all lengths together, applicants out of work got about 7% fewer positive replies. That overall result is borderline. Unadjusted, it could be chance; adjusted for differences between studies, it could not (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1380). Averaged over spells of 1 to 36 months, each extra month was linked with about 1.7% fewer positive replies. That average hides the first six months, which showed no clear cost (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1383).

The review has limits its authors name. It covers only peer-reviewed studies from one research database. It could not test whether the reason for being out of work changes the result (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1388).

When does a gap start to count?

The studies disagree. Four US tests from three research teams do not agree.

Study Sample Time out of work What happened to employer replies
D’hert and others 2026 (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1382) 16 field tests, 7 countries 1–6 months About 8% higher than applicants with a job; could be chance; not adjusted for study differences
D’hert and others 2026 (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1381) Same review 7–12 months A little lower in the review’s chart; could be chance; no number in the text
D’hert and others 2026 (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1382) Same review 13–18 months About 21% lower than applicants with a job; adjusted for study differences
D’hert and others 2026 (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1382) Same review 19–36 months About 27% lower than applicants with a job; adjusted for study differences
Kroft and others 2013 (Kroft et al. 2013) About 12,000 resumes, 100 US cities 1–36 months Callbacks fell from roughly 7% to 4% over the first 8 months, then stayed flat
Farber and others 2016 (Farber et al. 2016) About 12,000 applications by our count, 8 US cities 0–52 weeks No link between time out of work and callbacks
Farber and others 2019 (Farber et al. 2019) 8,488 US applications Up to 52 weeks 11.6% at 24 weeks, 9.1% at 52 weeks; no difference among shorter spells
Nunley and others 2017 (Nunley et al. 2017) About 9,400 resumes from recent college graduates, 7 US cities Out of work now or earlier, 3–12 months No link between time out of work and interview requests
Eriksson and Rooth 2014 (Eriksson and Rooth 2014) 8,466 applications, Sweden A past gap that had ended No clear effect

One US test found an early drop. Callbacks were about 45% lower at 8 months out of work than at 1 month. The 45% compares people newly out of work with people out longer. The test also sent 2,818 resumes from people with a job. The resumes from people with a job got fewer callbacks than resumes from people newly out of work. The authors call that result suggestive (Kroft et al. 2013).

A second US test is the strongest evidence against an early drop. Its authors found “no relationship between callback rates and the duration of unemployment” (Farber et al. 2016). Its applicants were all college-educated women applying for office support jobs. A follow-up by the same team found lower callbacks only at a full year out of work (Farber et al. 2019).

A third team sent about 9,400 resumes from recent college graduates to business jobs in seven US cities. Time out of work, whether 3, 6 or 12 months, showed no clear effect on interview requests (Nunley et al. 2017).

Why do the US tests differ? One team of study authors points to different designs, applicants, years and labor-market rules, the 2026 review reports (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1380). No study has tested which one explains the split.

Single tests of very short spells point both ways. The 2026 review lists one US study of people one month out of work. In that study, people one month out of work were less likely to get offers than applicants with a job. The review notes one exception: a cause outside the applicant’s control (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1373). We could open only that study’s summary, so we give no number from it.

A gap that has already ended is a different case. Most studies tested people out of work now. In Sweden, a past spell out of work had no clear effect on callbacks (Eriksson and Rooth 2014). The US test of recent graduates also found no effect of a past spell (Nunley et al. 2017). The 2026 review says field experiments on past gaps “report null findings” (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1380). Lab studies in that review all found a penalty for a past gap. The review notes lab raters probably knew what was being tested (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1380).

For you, a short gap, or an old gap followed by a job, showed no clear cost in most of these field tests.

Do employers filter out gaps with software?

Some say they do. A 2020 survey asked 2,275 executives in the US, UK and Germany about their hiring software. Among executives whose software ranks or filters applicants, 48% said it filtered middle-skills applicants on gaps over six months (Fuller et al. 2021, p. 22–23). That is what executives said, not a measurement of the software. It does not describe all employers. What such software can and can’t do is covered in Do ATS systems reject most resumes?.

Does explaining a gap help?

Sometimes, and sometimes not at all. The two main tests differ in more than the reason, so we can’t tell what made the difference.

Study Reason tested Result
Namingit and others 2021 (Namingit et al. 2021), 3,771 US resumes Illness with full recovery, said in the cover letter; the resume listed a cancer support group; gap of seven months or more Replies 25.6% with the reason, 23.3% with no reason; the published summary calls the gain significant
Kristal and others 2023 (Kristal et al. 2023), 9,022 UK applications Full-time childcare, 2.5-year gap 0.1 points apart from an unexplained gap
Weisshaar 2018 (Weisshaar 2018), 3,407 US applications Laid off, or left to care for children, 18-month gap Laid-off parents 8.8% to 9.7%; stay-at-home parents 4.9% to 5.4%
Bateson 2023 (Bateson 2023), 974 US adults, lab study Pandemic gap: laid off, child’s online school, or in and out of work Chosen about 20% less often, whatever the reason

The US health test had a third group, newly out of work, with replies of 27.4%. So the health reason won back about half of the drop between that group and the unexplained gap. Those three rates come from the authors’ 2018 summary and may differ in the printed paper. The illness was cancer, and the gap was seven months or more, by the trial’s registration. The resume also listed a cancer support group. The published summary says replies with the reason were “significantly higher” than with an unexplained gap (Namingit et al. 2021). The half is our arithmetic from the study’s three rates.

The UK test found no gain from a childcare line. It also tried listing years worked per job in place of dates. That format got 4.9 points more replies than an unexplained gap and 2.9 points more than no gap at all (Kristal et al. 2023). The authors note the test ran in the UK only.

Do parents who stayed home face a bigger penalty?

In the main US test, yes, and fathers as much as mothers. The test sent 3,407 made-up applications from parents to professional and managerial jobs. Some resumes showed a current job. Some said the parent was laid off 18 months ago. Some said the parent left work to care for children (Weisshaar 2018).

Stay-at-home parents got about half as many interview requests as laid-off parents. A later lab study by the same author asked 2,000 US adults to choose between made-up applicants. Positive information on past job performance and social skills wiped out the penalty for being out of work. It did not wipe out the penalty for caregiving (Weisshaar 2021). Those raters were members of the public, not hiring staff.

The 2026 review counts four studies of people who left the workforce. All four found a penalty. Two found it worse than for job loss, and two found no extra penalty (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1380).

This is bias by employers. It says nothing about how well a parent will do the job.

Does a stop-gap job or volunteering help?

A stop-gap job below your level did not help in three US tests. One sent 2,420 applications with different last-year histories. Men with a full-time job at their level got callbacks 10.4% of the time. Men in a job below their skill level got 4.7%, and men a year out of work got 4.2%. For women in the same test, a below-level job also cut callbacks, from 10.4% to 5.2%. A year out of work showed no clear cost for women. Temp agency work showed no clear cost for men or women (Pedulla 2016). In another test, a lower-level interim job cut callbacks from 9.8% to 8.5% (Farber et al. 2016). In the third, graduates working in a job that needs no degree got about 30% fewer interview requests. The comparison group held jobs that fit their degree (Nunley et al. 2017).

That is a result about the resume page, not about taking the job. A stop-gap job pays bills. Whether it goes on the page is your call; application forms still ask for full work history.

Volunteering did better in one test. In Belgium, resumes that listed volunteer work got about a third more interview invitations (Baert and Vujić 2018). That test did not place the volunteering inside a gap. We found no field test of volunteering during a gap.

Does a layoff look worse than other job loss?

Old US records hint that it can, compared with a plant closing. In surveys from 1984 and 1986, laid-off men lost about 4% more in pay at their next job than men whose plant closed. They also stayed out of work about 25% longer (Gibbons and Katz 1991). Those are real records, so the layoff is linked with the worse result, not shown to cause it. The data is forty years old.

In the one test that compared a layoff with caregiving, laid-off parents got more interview requests (Weisshaar 2018). We found no field test that compared naming a layoff with saying nothing.

What can you say about a health gap?

A few words is enough – no diagnosis. In the US, as of October 2026, an employer may not ask disability-related questions before a conditional job offer (US Equal Employment Opportunity Commission 1995). That federal rule covers employers with 15 or more employees (US Equal Employment Opportunity Commission 1990). State rules vary. This is not legal advice.

A line such as “a health matter, now resolved or well managed” names no condition. That wording is convention, not a tested phrase. The one field test of a health reason used a letter naming a physical illness with full recovery. The resume pointed to cancer (Namingit et al. 2021). No study we found tested a shorter health line.

Should you change dates to hide a gap?

No. Many employers check. A background-check company ran a survey in 2025. More than three-quarters of the businesses it asked had found mismatches in applicants’ details in the past 12 months. Work history was one of the most common kinds (HireRight 2025a). A later release said work history checks found mismatches most often in every region, named by 72% of respondents in Asia-Pacific (HireRight 2025b). The company sells those checks, so read its numbers with care.

A gap costs some replies at some employers. A false date found in a check is a mismatch on record.

What we don’t know

  • Why US tests disagree on short gaps (Kroft et al. 2013; Farber et al. 2016; Nunley et al. 2017).
  • How much 7 to 12 months out of work costs. The largest review charts a small drop that could be chance and prints no number (D’hert et al. 2026, p. 1381).
  • Whether a reason beats no reason, for anything other than illness and childcare. We searched the web and arXiv in October 2026 and found tests of those two only.
  • Whether these results hold now. Every field test here ran in 2020 or earlier.
  • Whether real hiring staff share the public’s view of pandemic gaps. The one study asked US adults (Bateson 2023).
  • How many employers’ software really filters gaps. The 48% figure is what executives said (Fuller et al. 2021).
  • Whether a past gap matters for older US workers or those without a degree. The US test of past gaps used recent graduates (Nunley et al. 2017).

What helps

  • Still searching? A search under way is not a gap to explain. In the largest review, the first six months out of work showed no clear cost (D’hert et al. 2026).
  • Gap of six months or more: a one-line reason is your call. It helped for one health reason and did nothing for childcare (Namingit et al. 2021; Kristal et al. 2023). The six-month line comes from a survey. Of executives whose software ranks or filters applicants, 48% said it filters middle-skill applicants on gaps over six months (Fuller et al. 2021).
  • Gap of a year or more: list real work, study or volunteering you did. Volunteering on a resume raised invitations in one test (Baert and Vujić 2018).
  • Keep health reasons to a few words. Covered US employers may not ask about the condition before a conditional offer, as of October 2026 (US Equal Employment Opportunity Commission 1995).
  • Laid off: naming it in one line is your call. It did better than a caregiving reason in one US test, but no test compared it with saying nothing (Weisshaar 2018).
  • Keep dates true on the resume and on every form. Background checks can compare them (HireRight 2025a).
  • Apply widely. Employers differ, so one rejection says little about the next.

How CEZ Job Finder uses this

  • CEZ Job Finder raises a gap once, at setup, only when it is six months or longer. A search still under way is never treated as a gap.
  • A reason line is your choice; the app says what the studies found and then does what you pick.
  • It asks for a few words only, never a diagnosis.
  • It never shifts dates to hide a gap, and application forms get your full work history.
  • The rules and their sources are in the program’s fair screening notes.
