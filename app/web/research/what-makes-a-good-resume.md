**Short answer**

- True first: employer, title, dates match records. Mismatches get found (Vendor survey) [@hireright-2025].
- Typos cost interviews: 2 errors, 7 points lower chance (Lab study) [@sterkens-2023].
- Long breaks cost replies; 1-6 months showed no clear cost (Big study) [@dhert-2024].
- Results over duties, 1-2 pages, last 10-15 years, no photo: career-guide convention, no study (Convention) [@mit-capd-resumes].

## What matters most on a resume?

A resume has four jobs, in this order: be true, show real results, fit the job, read easily. The order is our rule, not a study's finding. When two rules clash, the earlier one wins. A strong line that is not true fails the first job.

Most resume advice has never been tested in real hiring. A few rules have strong studies behind them, and many rest on career-guide agreement. Each section below says which kind of evidence backs the rule. How we grade evidence: [How we research](methods.md).

For you, the rules with studies behind them deserve the most care: accuracy, typos and breaks.

## Does your resume have to match your records?

Yes. Employers check work history, and mismatches are common enough to get caught. In a 2025 survey by HireRight, a background-check company, more than three-quarters of employers found a mismatch in the past year [@hireright-2025]. The most common were undisclosed criminal records and education or work history that did not match [@hireright-2025].

HireRight sells background checks, and the survey's question wording is not published. The finding shows employers check, not how often a mismatch costs a job.

For you, the employer name, job title and dates should match what a past employer would confirm. Anything on the page can also come up in an interview.

## Should a resume list results or duties?

Career guides agree: show what you achieved, not only what the job asked of you. MIT's career office says to record "accomplishments and contributions, not just responsibilities" [@mit-capd-resumes]. Harvard's career office lists "not demonstrating results" among common resume mistakes [@harvard-ocs-resume].

The rule is convention. We found no study that tested results lines against duty lines in real hiring.

A result does not need a number. A line can say what changed, for whom, or how big the work was. A number you cannot explain in an interview hurts more than no number.

For you, rewrite duty lines into what you did and what came of it, using only facts you can back up.

## Should you use the words in the job posting?

Use the posting's words for skills you really have. Recruiters can search stored resumes for words, much like a web search [@greenhouse-search]. A skill you have but name differently may not come up in that search.

The rule rests on how hiring software is built, not on a study of interviews. We found no large study showing that matching a posting's words gets more interviews. The common claim that software rejects most resumes does not hold up [@levinson-2012-cio]. Where that claim came from: [Do hiring systems reject most resumes?](ats-rejection-myth.md).

For you, a skill the posting asks for and you lack is a gap to know about. Adding the word without the skill invites questions you cannot answer.

## How far back should a resume go?

Career guides suggest the last 10-15 years in detail. One guide tells senior candidates to list work "up to the last 10-15 years" [@indeed-experience-2025]. Put the work most relevant to the job first.

The rule is convention, with no study behind it. Older jobs can stay as short lines, so the page shows no false hole in the middle.

For you, give recent, relevant work the most room, and shorten older roles rather than hiding them.

## Does a gap in your resume hurt?

Long gaps cost replies from employers; short ones showed no clear cost. A 2024 review combined field experiments with about 67,000 made-up applicants in 7 countries [@dhert-2024, p. 8]. A gap of 1-6 months showed no clear effect on positive replies [@dhert-2024, p. 15]. A gap of 13-18 months cut positive replies by about 21% [@dhert-2024, p. 15]. A gap of 19-36 months cut them by about 27% [@dhert-2024, p. 15]. The review is a discussion paper, not yet peer-reviewed.

A US field experiment found the same shape. Replies to about 12,000 made-up resumes fell sharply over the first 8 months out of work, then stayed flat [@kroft-2013].

Some screening software also filters gaps. In a 2020 survey, 48% of executives whose software ranks or filters applicants said it filtered on gaps over 6 months [@fuller-2021, p. 22-23]. Those are executives' answers about their own software, not a measurement.

For you, gaps under about 6 months are a weak worry. Longer ones are worth handling with care, as below.

## Does explaining a gap help?

The studies disagree. In one US field experiment, a cover letter saying the gap was an illness, now recovered, did a little better than no reason [@namingit-2021]. Replies were 25.6% with the reason and 23.3% without it [@namingit-2021]. The open summary does not say whether that difference could be chance.

A larger UK experiment found no gain from a reason. A 2.5-year gap explained as full-time childcare did no better than an unexplained gap [@kristal-2023]. The same study tried listing years worked per job instead of dates. Listing years raised replies by about 8% over resumes with no gap at all [@kristal-2023].

For you, a one-line reason is a cheap, honest step, but no study shows it reliably helps. Bias against gaps is the employer's, not a flaw in you. Application forms often ask for exact dates anyway.

## Do typos really matter?

Yes. Typos are among the best-tested resume rules. In a 2023 study, 445 recruiters in Belgium rated made-up graduate resumes [@sterkens-2023]. Two spelling errors lowered the rated chance of an interview by 7.3 points [@sterkens-2023]. Five errors lowered it by 18.5 points [@sterkens-2023, p. 10].

About half of that penalty came from recruiters judging the applicant as less careful, less able or worse with people [@sterkens-2023]. The study asked recruiters to rate resumes, not to hire, so it is a lab study.

For you, a careful proofread, by you and someone else, is one of the cheapest fixes there is.

## How long should a resume be?

One page is the student rule; two pages is common with more experience. MIT's career office says to stick to one page "unless you have extensive experience or an advanced degree" [@mit-capd-resumes]. That is convention.

The study most often quoted for two pages is weak. ResumeGo, a resume-writing company, ran a hiring simulation with 482 recruiters and managers [@resumego-2018]. Two-page resumes were picked 5,375 times out of 7,712 [@resumego-2018]. The page does not say whether the two-page resumes also held more experience.

Typeface changes page count more than people expect. Our measurement: the same resume ran 2 pages in a font fitting 98 characters per line. A wider font fitting 91 characters per line ran 3 pages, with 19 lines with a few words alone. Nothing else changed.

For you, use the length your relevant experience fills, and pick a narrow, plain font before cutting true content.

## What should a resume leave off?

US career guides say to leave off a photo and personal details. MIT's career office says not to include age, religion, health or marital status [@mit-capd-resumes]. MIT also says photos "are generally not preferred" on US resumes [@mit-capd-resumes].

A photo invites judgments about looks. In an Israeli field experiment, 5,312 resumes went to 2,656 real job ads, some with a photo [@ruffle-2015]. Attractive men got more replies with a photo [@ruffle-2015]. Women without a photo got the most replies of all women [@ruffle-2015]. Photos are common in Israel, so US results may differ.

A full street address adds little a US employer needs before an interview; city and state is the usual advice. That rule is convention.

For you, leave off the photo, birth date, marital status and street address. City and state are enough.

## Does layout matter?

A plain, one-column page is the safe choice. In a 2018 eye-tracking test by Ladders, a job site, top-rated resumes had simple layouts and clear headings [@ladders-2018]. Poor ones had clutter, several columns and missing headings [@ladders-2018]. The press release does not say how many recruiters took part.

Our measurement shows how layout trips up software. A heading with letters spaced apart came back as "EXP E R I ENC E" from one way of converting a PDF. Dates set at the right edge of a line were read after the job's bullet points. A degree and school on one line filled Workday's school box wrongly and left its degree box empty.

For you, use one column, plain headings, normal letter spacing and black text, and put each item on its own line.

## Which resume advice is not backed by evidence?

- **"Recruiters read a resume for 6 seconds."** The figure comes from a 2012 eye-tracking test of 30 recruiters by a job site [@ladders-2012]. It measured a first fit-or-not glance, not the whole reading.
- **"75% of resumes are rejected by software."** A 2012 sales claim with no method behind it [@levinson-2012-cio]. The full story: [Where the number came from](ats-rejection-myth.md).
- **"Put a number on every line."** The advice pushes people to make up numbers. A number you cannot explain hurts in an interview.
- **"Add the posting's keywords."** Only for skills you have. A listed skill you lack gets asked about.
- **0-100 resume scores from checker tools.** We found no published method linking these scores to interviews.
- **Two-column or photo templates.** They look modern but read worse by software and invite bias, as above.
- **Letters spaced apart in headings.** Some PDF readers split the word apart, as above.

## What we don't know

- Whether results lines beat duty lines in real hiring. No study found.
- Whether matching a posting's words gets more interviews. No large study found.
- Whether a gap reason helps: one small US study says a little, one large UK study says no.
- Whether one page or two works better for experienced people. The only test found is a vendor's simulation.
- How AI screening tools treat any of these rules. See [Can employers tell if AI wrote your resume?](ai-written-resumes.md).

## What helps

- Make every employer, title and date match your records [@hireright-2025].
- Proofread, and have someone else proofread too [@sterkens-2023].
- Keep gaps short where you can. Longer breaks cost more [@dhert-2024].
- Leave off a photo and personal details [@mit-capd-resumes].
- Use a plain, one-column page with clear headings [@ladders-2018].

## How CEZ Job Finder uses this

- Never changes an employer, title or date, except to fix a real mistake.
- Checks spelling and US forms on every resume.
- Uses a posting's words only where your experience backs them.
- Makes a one-column page, black text, no photo, and checks it reads back cleanly.
- Asks you for real numbers; never makes one up.
