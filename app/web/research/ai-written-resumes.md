**Short answer**

- Most readers can't reliably tell AI writing from human writing (Lab study, 4,600 people) [@jakesch-2023].
- People who use AI often for writing spot it far better (Lab study, small) [@russell-2025].
- Detector tools misfire, and wrongly flag non-native English writers (Lab study; maker's docs) [@liang-2023; @openai-2023].
- Hiring managers say they can tell and dislike generic AI text (Vendor surveys) [@insight-global-2025; @resume-genius-2026].
- Writing help raised hires in one big test, but AI-tailored letters now count for less (Big study; Real records, preprints) [@wiles-2025; @cui-2025].

## Can people tell if a resume was written by AI?

Most people can't, in the largest test. In 2023, 4,600 people judged short self-descriptions written by people or by AI [@jakesch-2023]. The texts included freelancer profiles of the kind used to win work [@jakesch-2023]. People picked the right source only 50% to 52% of the time, about as good as a coin toss [@jakesch-2023]. Paying people for right answers barely helped, at 51.6% [@jakesch-2023]. Telling them after each answer whether they were right gave 51.2% [@jakesch-2023].

People relied on wrong clues. They took first-person words, contractions and family topics as signs of a human writer [@jakesch-2023]. The AI models in that study were older ones, GPT-2 and GPT-3.

The strongest counter-evidence comes from a 2025 study of practiced readers. Nine paid readers labeled 300 articles as human or AI [@russell-2025]. Four readers who rarely used AI for writing did about as well as chance [@russell-2025]. They flagged 56.7% of AI articles but also 52.5% of human ones [@russell-2025]. Five readers who used AI for writing often were far better [@russell-2025]. Their majority vote got 299 of the 300 articles right [@russell-2025]. Their most common clue was "AI vocabulary," words like "vibrant" and "crucial" [@russell-2025]. Those were news-style articles, not resumes.

For you, a reader who uses AI often may well notice AI wording. Most readers will guess, and some will guess wrong about text you wrote yourself.

## Do AI detectors work on resumes?

Not reliably, and they can be unfair. OpenAI released its own AI-text detector in January 2023 [@openai-2023]. In its maker's test, the detector caught 26% of AI text [@openai-2023]. It wrongly flagged human text 9% of the time [@openai-2023]. OpenAI withdrew the detector in July 2023, citing "its low rate of accuracy" [@openai-2023].

A 2023 study ran seven detectors on essays all written by people [@liang-2023]. The detectors were nearly perfect on essays by US eighth graders [@liang-2023]. On essays by non-native English writers, they flagged 61% as AI on average [@liang-2023]. 97.8% of those essays were flagged by at least one detector [@liang-2023]. Plainer word choice made human writing look like AI to the detectors [@liang-2023].

Detectors have changed since then. In the 2025 test above, one paid detector matched the practiced readers [@russell-2025]. Two free, openly released detectors caught as little as 6.7% and 23.3% of AI articles that had been reworded [@russell-2025].

We found no study that tests detectors on resumes. We also found no study showing how many employers run them.

For you, a detector's "AI" label is weak evidence. Plain, simple English can trip it.

## What do hiring managers say about AI-written resumes?

They say they can tell, and many say they care. Both surveys below come from companies in the hiring or resume business.

In an October 2024 survey of 1,005 US HR and hiring leaders, 88% said they can tell when applicants use AI [@insight-global-2025]. 54% said they would care if a resume or cover letter was written by AI [@insight-global-2025]. 46% said they would not care [@insight-global-2025].

A 2026 survey of 1,000 US hiring managers was run for a resume-builder company [@resume-genius-2026]. 80% said they can often tell when AI wrote a resume [@resume-genius-2026]. 76% said AI-written resumes make it harder to see what a person actually did [@resume-genius-2026]. 72% said heavy reliance on AI makes applicants seem less skilled [@resume-genius-2026].

The signs they named were mostly about quality. The top ones were unnatural phrasing (51%), repetitive or generic wording (44%) and vague or inflated descriptions (41%) [@resume-genius-2026]. 32% named formatting habits such as long dashes [@resume-genius-2026].

Saying you can spot AI is not the same as spotting it. In one lab test, readers who rarely used AI felt sure but did no better than chance [@russell-2025].

For you, the warning signs managers name are generic, vague and inflated lines. Those are worth cutting whoever wrote them.

## Does using AI on your resume hurt your chances?

Writing help raised hiring in the largest real test. That test used spelling and grammar help, not today's chat AI.

In 2021, an online freelance platform ran an experiment with 480,948 new jobseekers [@wiles-2025]. Half got automatic suggestions on spelling, grammar and wording for their profile [@wiles-2025]. Those who got the help were hired 8% more often [@wiles-2025]. They were paid 10% more per hour, too [@wiles-2025]. Employers rated the people they hired just as highly afterward [@wiles-2025]. The authors' reading: clearer writing helped employers see what people could do [@wiles-2025].

Chat AI that writes the whole text is different. Two 2025 studies looked at one freelance platform after it added an AI letter writer in April 2023 [@cui-2025; @galdin-silbert-2025]. Both are preprints, not yet peer-reviewed.

The first studied 5 million cover letters to over 100,000 jobs [@cui-2025]. People who used the AI tool got more callbacks, by 3.56 percentage points [@cui-2025]. That gain was not certain enough to rule out chance, and it faded after two months [@cui-2025]. Before the tool, a letter that closely matched the job post went with more callbacks. After the tool, that link was 51% weaker [@cui-2025]. Employers leaned more on each worker's past reviews instead [@cui-2025]. Longer editing of the AI draft went with a higher chance of a job offer [@cui-2025]. Most AI letters were sent with little or no editing [@cui-2025].

The second studied about 2.7 million applications to coding jobs [@galdin-silbert-2025]. Before chat AI, employers paid more for workers whose applications fit the job closely [@galdin-silbert-2025]. After chat AI, that premium fell sharply [@galdin-silbert-2025]. The authors then used a model of the market to ask what happens with no such signal. In that model, the most able workers were hired 19% less often [@galdin-silbert-2025]. The least able were hired 14% more often [@galdin-silbert-2025]. Those two numbers come from the model, not from counting real hires.

For you, polishing your own wording helped in a large test. A letter or resume that only echoes the job post now tells employers less. Your real record carries more weight.

## Does AI wording help with AI screeners?

In one lab test, it did. A 2025 study took 2,245 real resumes and had AI models rewrite each summary section [@xu-2025]. Each AI model then picked between the person's own summary and one the same model wrote [@xu-2025]. Eight of the nine models picked their own version more often [@xu-2025]. In simulated hiring for 24 jobs, applicants using the screener's own AI were 23% to 60% more likely to be shortlisted [@xu-2025]. That is a lab test, not a real employer's system.

For you, this cuts both ways. An AI screener may like AI wording, while a human reader may dislike it. Clear, specific lines are the safest bet for both.

## What words make writing sound like AI?

Some words became much more common after chat AI arrived. A 2025 study counted words in over 15 million science paper summaries from 2010 to 2024 [@kobak-2025]. "Delves" appeared 28 times more often in 2024 than its earlier trend predicted [@kobak-2025]. "Underscores" rose 13.8 times and "showcasing" 10.7 times [@kobak-2025]. Common words like "crucial" and "potential" also jumped [@kobak-2025]. The authors estimate at least 13.5% of 2024 summaries were processed with AI [@kobak-2025]. That study is about science writing, not resumes. It shows which words AI overuses, not whether employers notice them.

For you, words like "delve," "showcase" and "pivotal" add no facts. Cutting them makes a line clearer, whoever wrote it.

## What we don't know

- How often employers run AI detectors on resumes. We found no study measuring it.
- How well detectors work on resumes, which are short and full of lists. No study found that tests this.
- Whether hiring managers who say they can tell actually can. The surveys report what they say.
- Whether today's chat AI helps or hurts resumes in real hiring. The large field test used spelling and grammar help, in 2021 [@wiles-2025].
- Whether the freelance platform results hold for regular jobs. Both studies come from one platform [@cui-2025; @galdin-silbert-2025].
- We searched the web, arXiv and news in October 2026 for studies on these gaps.

## What helps

- Use AI for wording, not facts. Clearer writing raised hires in the largest test [@wiles-2025].
- Put in your own facts and numbers. Employers lean on what AI can't make up, like your track record [@cui-2025].
- Edit what AI drafts. More editing time went with more job offers [@cui-2025].
- Cut generic, vague and inflated lines. Hiring managers name these as AI signs [@resume-genius-2026].
- Be able to explain every line. Anything on the page can come up in an interview.
- Never let AI invent a skill, job or number. That is a false claim, whoever typed it.
