**Short answer**

- Most forms still ask: 96 of 143 US forms had a cover letter box, nearly all optional (Our measurement, October 2026).
- A tailored letter beat no letter on callbacks in a 2019 test of 7,287 applications (vendor study, not peer-reviewed) [@resumego-2020].
- After an AI letter tool arrived, letters that matched the job post went with callbacks 51% less (Real records, preprint) [@cui-2025].
- What still counts: your own reasons and facts only you know; edit any AI draft (Real records; convention) [@cui-2025; @mit-capd-ai-cover-letters].

## Do employers still ask for cover letters?

Most application forms we checked still had a cover letter box. Our measurement read 143 application forms from US job postings in October 2026. Of those, 96 of the 143 forms had a cover letter box, or 67%. The box was optional on 91 of those 96 forms; 2 required one, and 3 we could not check because the posting had closed. By employer, 71 of the 104 employers had a cover letter box on at least one form.

The form system mattered more than the job. Our measurement found a box on 85 of 96 Greenhouse forms. It found a box on 0 of 19 Lever forms, which offer an open "additional information" box instead. It found a box on 3 of 15 Ashby forms and 7 of 12 Workable forms. The forms, the counting code and the limits are on [our data page](cover-letter-boxes-2026-10.md).

A box on the form does not mean anyone reads what goes in it. No study we found measures how often optional letters are opened.

For you, a cover letter box is common, and it is almost always optional.

## Does a cover letter get you more interviews?

In the one large test we found, a tailored letter got more callbacks than none. ResumeGo, a resume-writing company, sent 7,287 made-up applications from July 2019 to January 2020. The applications went to jobs on ZipRecruiter, Glassdoor and Indeed. Each went out with no letter, a generic letter or a letter tailored to the job. Within 30 days, 10.7% of applications with no letter got a callback. Generic letters got 12.5%, and tailored letters got 16.4%, about half again as many as no letter [@resumego-2020].

```bars
Callbacks within 30 days, by cover letter, 2019-2020 (vendor study, 7,287 made-up applications, not peer-reviewed) [@resumego-2020]
Application sent with | Callback rate
No cover letter | 10.7%
Generic cover letter | 12.5%
Cover letter tailored to the job | 16.4%
```

The study has real limits. ResumeGo sells resume and letter writing, and the study was not peer-reviewed. The page gives no group sizes, no job types and no test of whether the gap could be chance. The test ran before chat AI could write a letter in seconds [@resumego-2020].

No peer-reviewed field test of letter against no letter turned up in our search. The ResumeGo result is the best direct evidence, and it is weak.

## Do recruiters actually read cover letters?

Recruiters say they do, in surveys run by companies that sell letter help. In ResumeGo's survey of 236 recruiters and hiring managers, 87% said they read cover letters. A smaller share, 65%, said letters had real weight in who gets an interview or a job. Most readers were quick: 32% said they spend under 10 seconds, and 52% said 10 seconds to a minute. Only 26% said they hold a missing optional letter against an applicant [@resumego-2020-survey].

A 2025 survey of 200 US recruiters and hiring managers was run for ResumeLab, which sells a letter builder. In it, 77% said they read cover letters even when not required. The same share said they prefer applicants who sent an optional letter. Only 13% said they would still consider an application missing a required letter. The page gives no survey dates or question wording [@resumelab-2025].

Both surveys come from sellers of the thing they ask about. Both report what recruiters say, not what they do. A recruiter who says letters matter may still skip most of them.

## Does AI change what a cover letter tells employers?

AI letters raised callbacks a little, but letters now tell employers less. The best evidence comes from one freelance job platform that added an AI letter writer in April 2023 [@cui-2025].

Researchers studied 5 million cover letters on that platform. Among those who could use it, 62% did. Having the tool raised callbacks by 0.43 per 100 letters, from a base of about 7 per 100. Before the tool, a letter that closely matched the job post went with more callbacks. After the tool, that link was 51% weaker. The link between a close match and a job offer fell 79%. Employers leaned a little more on each worker's past reviews instead. The study found no change in overall hiring [@cui-2025].

Most AI letters went out unedited. Over three in four AI letters were sent within a minute of being made. Only about 5% were edited for five minutes or more. Workers who spent longer editing their AI draft got more job offers. That link is not proof that editing caused the offers [@cui-2025]. The study is a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed.

A second study on the same platform looked at about 2.7 million applications to coding jobs. Before chat AI, employers paid more for workers whose written applications fit the job closely. After chat AI, that premium mostly disappeared, and a close fit no longer predicted finishing the job well. The authors then used a model of the market to ask who wins and loses. In that model, the most able workers were hired 19% less often and the least able 14% more often. Those two numbers come from the model, not from counting real hires [@galdin-silbert-2025].

Both studies cover one freelance platform, not regular jobs with a recruiter. Both point the same way: a letter anyone could have made with AI tells employers little.

For you, a letter that only echoes the job post now carries less weight. What you add yourself is what still counts.

## Can employers tell an AI-written cover letter?

Hiring managers say they can, and about half say they would mind. In a 2024 vendor survey of 1,005 US hiring leaders, 88% said they can tell when applicants use AI. Of those leaders, 54% said they would care if a resume or cover letter was written by AI [@insight-global-2025].

Saying you can tell is not the same as telling. Whether people and AI detectors really spot AI writing is covered in [Can employers tell if AI wrote your resume?](ai-written-resumes.md).

Each study answers a different question. Only the first tests whether a letter helps; the rest show what letters signal or what recruiters say.

## What we don't know

- Whether a cover letter still raises callbacks now that AI can write one. We found no study of letter against no letter since 2020.
- How often optional letters are opened at all. The surveys report what recruiters say.
- Whether the freelance platform results hold for regular jobs. Both AI studies come from one platform [@cui-2025; @galdin-silbert-2025].
- Whether editing an AI draft causes better results, or only goes with them [@cui-2025].
- How forms on Workday, iCIMS and other large systems handle letters. Our count covered five other form systems.
- We searched the web, arXiv and Google Scholar in October 2026 for field tests of cover letters and for surveys with a published method.

## What helps

- Write a letter when the form asks for one. A tailored letter beat no letter in the one test we found [@resumego-2020].
- Say in your own words why you want this job. Career guides agree the reason must come from you; that is convention, not a study [@mit-capd-ai-cover-letters].
- Add something your resume doesn't show; don't repeat it. Career guides agree on this too [@mit-capd-cover-letters].
- Use AI to fix spelling and wording in your own draft. That kind of help raised hires in a large test of job profiles, not letters [@wiles-2025].
- If AI drafts, edit it with your own facts. Longer editing went with more job offers, in one study [@cui-2025].
- Never let AI invent a skill, number or reason. A guide warns AI can oversell you or make up claims [@mit-capd-ai-cover-letters].
- Keep it to one page. That is convention [@mit-capd-cover-letters].
