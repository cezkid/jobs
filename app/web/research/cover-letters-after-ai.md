**Short answer**

- Many forms have a box: 96 of 143 US forms we read, mostly Greenhouse; nearly all optional (Our measurement, October 2026).
- A tailored letter beat no letter on callbacks in a 2019-20 test of 7,287 applications (vendor study, not peer-reviewed) [@resumego-2020].
- After an AI letter tool arrived, the link between a letter matching the job post and callbacks fell 51% (Real records, preprint) [@cui-2025].
- What still counts: your own reasons and facts only you know; edit any AI draft (Real records; convention) [@cui-2025; @mit-capd-ai-cover-letters].

## Do employers still ask for cover letters?

Many application forms still have a cover letter box. Our measurement read 143 application forms from US job postings in October 2026. Of those, 96 had a cover letter box (67%). The box was optional on 91 of those 96 forms; 2 required one, and 3 we could not check because the hiring system no longer showed the posting. By employer, 71 of the 104 employers had a cover letter box on at least one form.

The form system made the biggest difference we saw. Our measurement found a box on 85 of 96 Greenhouse forms, where it sits with the basic boxes such as name and email. It found a box on 3 of 15 Ashby forms and 7 of 12 Workable forms. Our measurement found 0 of 19 Lever forms with a box named cover letter. Some Lever forms show an open "additional information" box whose hint says to add a cover letter; we did not count those. Job field and form system overlap in our sample, so we can't fully separate the two. The forms, the counting code and the limits are on [our data page](cover-letter-boxes-2026-10.md).

A much larger count from the same job search points the same way. The maker of freehire.me, the job search our sample came from, says 209,297 of 402,117 open postings with a captured form have a letter box, about half. That count uses the same read of forms as ours, and its notes give no method or date [@freehire-letter-count].

A box on the form does not mean anyone reads what goes in it. No study we found measures how often optional letters are opened.

For you, a cover letter box is common, and it is almost always optional.

## Does a cover letter get you more interviews?

In the one field test we found, a tailored letter got more callbacks than none. ResumeGo, a resume-writing company, sent 7,287 made-up applications from July 2019 to January 2020. The applications went to jobs on ZipRecruiter, Glassdoor and Indeed. Each went out with no letter, a generic letter or a letter tailored to the job. Within 30 days, 10.7% of applications with no letter got a callback. Generic letters got 12.5%, and tailored letters got 16.4%, about half again as many as no letter [@resumego-2020].

```bars
Callbacks within 30 days, by cover letter, 2019-2020 (vendor study, 7,287 made-up applications, not peer-reviewed) [@resumego-2020]
Application sent with | Callback rate
No cover letter | 10.7%
Generic cover letter | 12.5%
Cover letter tailored to the job | 16.4%
```

The study has real limits. ResumeGo sells resume writing, and the study was not peer-reviewed. The page gives no group sizes and no job types. It does not say the applications were split between groups at random. It gives no test of whether the gap could be chance. Where a form had a letter box, the no-letter group left it blank or wrote "N/A". The test ran before chat AI could write a letter in seconds [@resumego-2020].

No peer-reviewed field test of letter against no letter turned up in our search. We searched the web and arXiv in October 2026 for field tests of cover letters and for surveys with a published method. The ResumeGo result is the best direct evidence, and it is weak.

## Do recruiters actually read cover letters?

Recruiters say they do, in surveys run by companies that sell resume or letter help. In ResumeGo's survey of 236 recruiters and hiring managers, 87% said they read cover letters. A smaller share, 65%, said letters had real weight in who gets an interview or a job. Most readers were quick: 32% said they spend under 10 seconds, and 52% said 10 seconds to a minute. Only 26% said they hold a missing optional letter against an applicant [@resumego-2020-survey].

A survey of 200 US recruiters and hiring managers was run for ResumeLab, which sells a letter builder. Its page is dated 2025, but the same survey was on it by February 2020, before chat AI. In it, 77% said they read cover letters even when not required. Oddly, fewer, 74%, said they read them when required. Also 77% said they prefer applicants who sent an optional letter. Only 13% said they would still consider an application missing a required letter. The page gives no survey dates and says some answers were rephrased [@resumelab-2020].

Both surveys come from sellers of the thing they ask about. Both report what recruiters say, not what they do. A recruiter who says letters matter may still skip most of them.

## Does AI change what a cover letter tells employers?

AI letters raised callbacks a little for a short time, but letters now tell employers less. The best evidence comes from one freelance job platform that added an AI letter writer in April 2023 [@cui-2025].

Researchers studied 5 million cover letters on that platform. Among those who could use the tool, 62% used it at least once. Having the tool raised callbacks by 0.43 per 100 letters, from a base of about 7 per 100. That gain was statistically weak, and it faded after about two months. Before the tool, a letter that shared many words with the job post went with more callbacks. After the tool, that link was 51% weaker. The link between a close match and a job offer fell 79%. Employers leaned a little more on each worker's past reviews instead. The authors found no evidence of a change in overall hiring, and call that result early [@cui-2025].

Most AI letters got little or no editing. Over three in four AI letters were sent within a minute of being made. Only about 5% were sent after five minutes or more. Workers who spent longer editing their AI draft got more job offers. That link is not proof that editing caused the offers [@cui-2025]. The study is a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed.

A second team studied about 2.7 million applications to coding jobs on the same platform. Before chat AI, employers were willing to pay more for workers whose written applications fit the job closely. After chat AI, that premium mostly disappeared, and a close fit no longer predicted finishing the job well. The authors then used a model of the market to test an extreme case: letters that tell employers nothing about how able a worker is. In that case, the top fifth of workers were hired 19% less often and the bottom fifth 14% more often. Those numbers come from the model, not from counting real hires [@galdin-silbert-2025].

Both studies use the same platform's data, so they are not two independent tests. Neither covers regular jobs with a recruiter. Both point the same way: a letter anyone could have made with AI tells employers little.

For you, a letter that only echoes the job post now carries less weight. What you add yourself is what still counts.

## Can employers tell an AI-written cover letter?

Hiring managers say they can, and about half say they would mind. In a 2024 vendor survey of 1,005 US hiring leaders, 88% said they can tell when applicants use AI. Of those leaders, 54% said they would care if a resume or cover letter was written by AI [@insight-global-2025].

One small lab test points the other way. In a 2024 student thesis, 86 raters each scored six made-up cover letters. Letters written by ChatGPT got the highest hiring scores, above letters people wrote themselves. The scores barely moved after raters were told AI may have been used. The raters were screened only by two questions about themselves, and no one was really hired [@kleine-allekotte-2024].

Saying you can tell is not the same as telling. Whether people and AI detectors really spot AI writing is covered in [Can employers tell if AI wrote your resume?](ai-written-resumes.md).

Each study answers a different question. Only the first tests whether a letter helps; the rest show what letters signal or what people say.

## What we don't know

- Whether a cover letter still raises callbacks now that AI can write one. We found no study of letter against no letter since 2020.
- How often optional letters are opened at all. The surveys report what recruiters say.
- Whether the freelance platform results hold for regular jobs. Both AI studies come from one platform [@cui-2025; @galdin-silbert-2025].
- Whether editing an AI draft causes better results, or only goes with them [@cui-2025].
- How forms on Workday, iCIMS and other large systems handle letters. Our count covered five other form systems.

## What helps

- Write a letter when the form asks for one. A tailored letter beat no letter in the one test we found [@resumego-2020].
- Say in your own words why you want this job. MIT's career office says that story must come from you; that is convention, not a study [@mit-capd-ai-cover-letters].
- Add something your resume doesn't show; don't repeat it. MIT's career office gives the same advice [@mit-capd-cover-letters].
- Use AI to fix spelling and wording in your own draft. That kind of help raised hires in a large test of job profiles, not letters [@wiles-2025].
- If AI drafts, edit it with your own facts. Longer editing went with more job offers, in one study [@cui-2025].
- Never let AI invent a skill, number or reason. A guide warns AI can oversell you or make up claims [@mit-capd-ai-cover-letters].
- Keep it to one page. That is convention [@mit-capd-cover-letters].
