# Review: Hiring discrimination: what studies show, what helps (`hiring-bias-what-helps.md`)

Verdict **revise**: 1 high, 9 medium, 13 low findings. Nearly every number the draft takes from
its 22 sources matches the source. The problems are in what is left out and in what the numbers
are made to carry. The court-certificate advice (table, a section paragraph) rests on a 2016
pilot; the same lead author's larger 2021 test found the certificate did not help, and the draft
does not mention it. The Short answer and the table lead with the 36% gap and leave out that the
largest and newest test found about 9%. "Apply widely" is our own inference, yet it sits in the
description without that caveat and in the table beside "Big study". The anonymous-resume section
gives only the two trials that backfired, while both of those sources say the evidence is mixed.
One sentence states the 9% on the wrong base, one says US tests found "no drop at 40" while a
study cited in this same article found one, and one says bias after the callback was "larger"
on a figure that does not show it.

## Findings

### High

- **H1. The certificate advice rests on a pilot that its own lead author's larger test did not
  bear out.** Lines 31, 103 (rows 20, 72, 73). leasure-2016 calls its results "promising
  preliminary". Leasure and Kaminski 2021 (J. Empirical Legal Studies 18(4), 849-875, doi
  10.1111/jels.12297; abstract read) ran two field experiments on the same Ohio certificate:
  holders "fared no better than those with an identical criminal record and no certificate" and
  got significantly fewer callbacks than people with no record. A reader with a record who acts
  on the table spends time and court fees on a step the newer, larger evidence says did not
  change callbacks. The draft also read the 2016 study only as an abstract on a third party's
  page, and nobody read its size. Fix: add the 2021 study to `sources.yml` (open more than the
  abstract if an open copy exists; otherwise say "summary only"). Line 103 -> "Court
  certificates have mixed evidence. A 2016 pilot in Ohio found a certificate more than tripled
  interview invitations or offers [@leasure-2016]. A larger 2021 test of the same certificate
  found holders did no better than people with the same record and no certificate
  [@leasure-kaminski-2021]. We read both studies' published summaries only." Table cell -> "Know
  what the question may ask; certificates: mixed evidence". Move the question to "What we don't
  know" as well. The same claim stands in `Guides/Unfair hiring - what's known, what helps.md`
  line 91 ("helps ... nearly closed the gap") and `app/docs/resume/fair-screening.md` line 104
  ("Certificates of relief help") - the revise bead brings both into line. status: fixed

### Medium

- **M1. The 9% is on the wrong base.** Line 40 (row 30). Kline, Rose and Walters: 2.1 points is
  "9% of the Black mean contact rate". Fix: "Names read as Black got 2.1 points fewer replies.
  Put the other way, names read as white got about 9% more." status: fixed
- **M2. The lead figure is the larger, older one, and "the same resume" is not exact.** Lines
  15, 28 (rows 5, 13). The Short answer and the table give only 36%. The largest test, and the
  newest fieldwork cited, found about 9%; its authors say past work "typically found larger
  proportional effects" and offer reasons (very large employers, high reply rates). A reader who
  sees only 36% gets the top of the range. The 2017 review also pools in-person tests with
  matched testers, so the applicants were equally qualified, not "with the same resume". Fix:
  Short answer -> "Names: across 28 US field tests from 1989 to 2015, white applicants got about
  36% more callbacks than equally qualified Black applicants, with no change over time (Big
  study) [@quillian-2017]. The largest test, at 108 big employers, found about 9% (Big study)
  [@kline-2022]." Table: add the 9% to the effect cell with its cite. Body line 40: add one
  sentence with the authors' two reasons. status: fixed
- **M3. "Apply widely" is carried further than its caveat.** Lines 3, 17, 28, 32, 48, 52, 127
  (rows 3, 4, 8, 14, 22, 35, 39, 86). Line 52 says honestly that no test measured it. But the
  description says it "fits the evidence best" with no caveat; the table puts it under "What
  helps" beside "Big study"; and the AI row gives "apply widely" with no source at all - the
  cited AI tests say nothing on it, and employers that share one screening model would not
  differ. The finding itself is also softer than "a few firms": the paper says most firms show
