**Short answer**

- Short gaps: up to 6 months out of work showed no clear cost in a 2026 review of 16 field tests (Big study) [@dhert-2026].
- Long gaps: 13 months or more cut employer replies by about a fifth or more in that review; US tests disagree on when it starts (Big studies) [@dhert-2026; @kroft-2013; @farber-2016].
- A reason line: a health reason did a little better than silence in one US test; a childcare reason changed nothing in a UK test (Small study; Big study) [@namingit-2021; @kristal-2023].
- Never change dates: background checks compare them (Vendor survey) [@hireright-2025]. The bias is the employer's, not a flaw in you.

## Does a gap in your work history cost you interviews?

A long gap often does. A short one showed no clear cost in the biggest review so far.

That review came out in 2026. It combined 16 field experiments with almost 67,000 made-up applicants in seven countries. Each experiment sent employers resumes that differed only in time out of work. The review compared applicants out of work now with applicants who had a job [@dhert-2026, p. 1357].

Length made the difference. Up to six months out of work, positive replies were about 8% higher than for applicants with a job. That difference could be chance. From 13 to 18 months, replies were about 21% lower. From 19 to 36 months, they were about 27% lower [@dhert-2026, p. 1382]. The review's summary says the harm becomes noticeable after about twelve months.

The review has limits its authors name. It covers only peer-reviewed studies from one research database. It could not test whether the reason for being out of work changes the result [@dhert-2026, p. 1388].

## When does a gap start to count?

The studies disagree. Three large US tests give three different answers.

| Study | Sample | Time out of work | What happened to employer replies |
|---|---|---|---|
| D'hert and others 2026 [@dhert-2026, p. 1382] | 16 field tests, 7 countries | 1-6 months | About 8% higher than applicants with a job; could be chance |
| D'hert and others 2026 [@dhert-2026, p. 1382] | Same review | 13-18 months | About 21% lower than applicants with a job |
| D'hert and others 2026 [@dhert-2026, p. 1382] | Same review | 19-36 months | About 27% lower than applicants with a job |
| Kroft and others 2013 [@kroft-2013] | About 12,000 resumes, 100 US cities | 1-36 months | Callbacks fell from roughly 7% to 4% over the first 8 months, then stayed flat |
| Farber and others 2016 [@farber-2016] | About 12,000 applications, 8 US cities | 0-52 weeks | No link between time out of work and callbacks |
| Farber and others 2019 [@farber-2019] | 8,488 US applications | Up to 52 weeks | 11.6% at 24 weeks, 9.1% at 52 weeks; no difference among shorter spells |
| Eriksson and Rooth 2014 [@eriksson-rooth-2014] | 8,466 applications, Sweden | A past gap that had ended | No clear effect |

One US test found an early drop. Callbacks were about 45% lower at 8 months out of work than at 1 month. That test compared people newly out of work with people out longer, not with people in a job [@kroft-2013].

A second US test is the strongest evidence against an early drop. Its authors found "no relationship between callback rates and the duration of unemployment" [@farber-2016]. Its applicants were all college-educated women applying for office support jobs. A follow-up by the same team found lower callbacks only at a full year out of work [@farber-2019].

Nobody has shown why these tests differ. They used different jobs, years and applicants.

A gap that has already ended is a different case. Most studies tested people out of work now. In Sweden, a past spell out of work had no clear effect on callbacks [@eriksson-rooth-2014]. The 2026 review says field experiments on past gaps "report null findings" [@dhert-2026, p. 1380].

For you, a short gap, or an old gap followed by a job, showed no clear cost in these tests.

## Do employers filter out gaps with software?

Some say they do. A 2020 survey asked 2,275 executives in the US, UK and Germany about their hiring software. Among executives whose software ranks or filters applicants, 48% said it filtered middle-skills applicants on gaps over six months [@fuller-2021, p. 22-23]. That is what executives said, not a measurement of the software. It does not describe all employers. What such software can and can't do is covered in [Do ATS systems reject most resumes?](ats-rejection-myth.md).

## Does explaining a gap help?

Sometimes a little, and sometimes not at all. The reason given seems to matter more than giving one.

| Study | Reason tested | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Namingit and others 2021 [@namingit-2021], 3,771 US resumes | Illness with full recovery, said in the cover letter | Replies 25.6% with the reason, 23.3% with no reason; no test of chance in the open summary |
| Kristal and others 2023 [@kristal-2023], 9,022 UK applications | Full-time childcare, 2.5-year gap | 0.1 points apart from an unexplained gap |
| Weisshaar 2018 [@weisshaar-2018], 3,407 US applications | Laid off, or left to care for children, 18-month gap | Laid-off parents 8.8% to 9.7%; stay-at-home parents 4.9% to 5.4% |
| Bateson 2023 [@bateson-2023], 974 US adults, lab study | Pandemic gap: laid off, child's online school, or in and out of work | Chosen about 20% less often, whatever the reason |

The US health test had a third group, newly out of work, with replies of 27.4%. So the health reason won back about half of the drop between that group and the unexplained gap [@namingit-2021]. The half is our arithmetic from the study's three rates. The open summary does not say whether the difference could be chance.

The UK test found no gain from a childcare line. It also tried listing years worked per job in place of dates. That format got 4.9 points more replies than an unexplained gap and 2.9 points more than no gap at all [@kristal-2023]. The authors note the test ran in the UK only. US application forms usually ask for a month and year anyway.

## Do parents who stayed home face a bigger penalty?

In the main US test, yes, and fathers as much as mothers. The test sent 3,407 made-up applications from parents to professional and managerial jobs. Some resumes showed a current job. Some said the parent was laid off 18 months ago. Some said the parent left work to care for children [@weisshaar-2018].

```bars
Interview requests by work history, US parents, 2015-2016 (Big study, 3,407 made-up applications) [@weisshaar-2018]
Applicant | Interview requests
Mothers, in a job | 15.3%
Fathers, in a job | 14.6%
Mothers, laid off | 9.7%
Fathers, laid off | 8.8%
Mothers, stayed home with children | 4.9%
Fathers, stayed home with children | 5.4%
```

Stay-at-home parents got about half as many interview requests as laid-off parents. A later lab study by the same author asked 2,000 US adults to choose between made-up applicants. Proof of good past performance wiped out the penalty for being out of work. It did not wipe out the penalty for caregiving [@weisshaar-2021]. Those raters were members of the public, not hiring staff.

The 2026 review counts four studies of people who left the workforce. All four found a penalty. Two found it worse than for job loss, and two found no extra penalty [@dhert-2026, p. 1380].

This is bias by employers. It says nothing about how well a parent will do the job.

## Does a stop-gap job or volunteering help?

A stop-gap job below your level did not help in two US tests. One sent 2,420 applications with different last-year histories. Men with a full-time job at their level got callbacks 10.4% of the time. Men in a job below their skill level got 4.7%, and men a year out of work got 4.2% [@pedulla-2016]. In another test, a lower-level interim job cut callbacks from 9.8% to 8.5% [@farber-2016].

That is a result about the resume page, not about taking the job. A stop-gap job pays bills. Whether it goes on the page is your call; application forms still ask for full work history.

Volunteering did better in one test. In Belgium, resumes that listed volunteer work got about a third more interview invitations [@baert-vujic-2018]. That test did not place the volunteering inside a gap. We found no field test of volunteering during a gap.

## Does a layoff look worse than other job loss?

Old US records hint that it can, compared with a plant closing. In surveys from 1984 and 1986, laid-off men lost about 4% more in pay at their next job than men whose plant closed. They also stayed out of work about 25% longer [@gibbons-katz-1991]. Those are real records, so the layoff is linked with the worse result, not shown to cause it. The data is forty years old.

Among reasons tested on resumes, a layoff did best. Laid-off parents got more interview requests than parents who left to give care [@weisshaar-2018].

## What can you say about a health gap?

A few words is enough - no diagnosis. In the US, as of October 2026, an employer may not ask disability-related questions before a job offer [@eeoc-ada-preemployment]. This is not legal advice.

A line such as "a health matter, now resolved or well managed" names no condition. That wording is convention, not a tested phrase. The one field test of a health reason named the illness and said the applicant had fully recovered [@namingit-2021]. No study we found tested a shorter health line.

## Should you change dates to hide a gap?

No. Employers check. In a 2025 survey by a background-check company, more than three-quarters of employers said they found mismatches in applicants' details over 12 months. Work history was one of the most common kinds [@hireright-2025]. A later release said work history checks found mismatches most often in every region, at 72% of firms in Asia-Pacific [@hireright-2025-sept]. The company sells those checks, so read its numbers with care.

A gap costs some replies at some employers. A false date found in a check can cost the offer.

## What we don't know

- Why US tests disagree on short gaps [@kroft-2013; @farber-2016].
- Whether a one-line reason helps for reasons other than illness and childcare. We searched the web and arXiv in October 2026 and found tests of those two only.
- Whether these results hold now. Every field test here ran in 2020 or earlier.
- Whether real hiring staff share the public's view of pandemic gaps. The one study asked US adults [@bateson-2023].
- How many employers' software really filters gaps. The 48% figure is what executives said [@fuller-2021].
- Whether a past gap matters in the US. The clearest test of past gaps is Swedish [@eriksson-rooth-2014].

## What helps

- Still searching? A search under way is not a gap to explain. Short spells showed no clear cost in the largest review [@dhert-2026].
- Gap of six months or more: a one-line reason is your call. It helped a little for health and did nothing for childcare [@namingit-2021; @kristal-2023].
- Gap of a year or more: list real work, study or volunteering you did. Volunteering on a resume raised invitations in one test [@baert-vujic-2018].
- Keep health reasons to a few words. US employers may not ask for more before an offer, as of October 2026 [@eeoc-ada-preemployment].
- Laid off: say so in one line if it is true. It did better than a caregiving reason in one US test [@weisshaar-2018].
- Keep dates true on the resume and on every form. Checks compare them [@hireright-2025].
