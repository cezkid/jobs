**Short answer**

- Names: US employers called white applicants back about 36% more often than Black applicants with the same resume, with no change since 1989 (Big study) [@quillian-2017].
- A few employers: among 108 large US firms, the worst fifth caused nearly half of the lost replies (Big study) [@kline-2022].
- What helps: applying widely fits that finding best. No resume change has been shown to remove bias, and hiding names backfired in two trials (Small study, Lab study) [@behaghel-2015; @hiscox-2017].
- Never change a fact to dodge bias. The bias is the employer's, not a flaw in you.

## How common is hiring discrimination?

Hiring discrimination is common, and field tests have measured it for decades.

A field test sends employers made-up applications that differ in one detail, such as the name. A callback means the employer answers and asks for an interview or more details. The table shows the strongest study we found for each kind of bias.

| Bias | Strongest study | Size of the effect | What helps the applicant | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Name read as Black or Latino | 28 US field tests since 1989 [@quillian-2017] | White applicants got 36% more callbacks than Black applicants and 24% more than Latino applicants | Apply widely; initials or a name you go by are your call | Big study |
| Age | 17 field tests of older age [@lippens-2023] | About 42% fewer positive replies for older applicants | Leaving off old dates is your call; never a false date | Big study |
| Break in work | 16 field tests in 7 countries [@dhert-2026] | Breaks of 13 months or more cut replies by about a fifth or more | A short true reason line may help | Big study |
| Criminal record | About 15,000 applications in New Jersey and New York City [@agan-starr-2018] | Employers that asked were 63% more likely to call applicants with no record | Know what the question may ask; a court certificate where your state has one | Big study |
| AI screening | 14 AI models, 24,024 resume pairs each [@gao-2026] | Who is favored changes by model and version | No resume change shown to help; apply widely | Lab study, preprint |

## Do employers treat names differently?

Yes. Many field tests have found it, and the gap has not shrunk for Black applicants.

A 2017 review combined 28 US field tests run since 1989, with 55,842 applications. White applicants got 36% more callbacks than Black applicants with the same qualifications. They got 24% more than Latino applicants. The gap for Black applicants did not change over 25 years, and the gap for Latino applicants may have shrunk a little [@quillian-2017].

The largest single test sent more than 83,000 made-up applications to 108 of the largest US employers. About 24% of applications got a reply within 30 days. Names read as Black got 2.1 points fewer replies, about 9% below the rate for names read as white [@kline-2022, pp. 2-4].

The bias does not stop at the callback. A 2020 review found 12 field tests that followed applicants through to the job offer. Majority applicants got 53% more callbacks than minority applicants, and 145% more job offers [@quillian-2020]. We read that review's published summary only.

A 2026 preprint adds detail on where the gaps sit. Its authors sent 36,880 made-up applications from new college graduates to 9,220 US job ads. In management jobs, Black men, Black women, white women and Hispanic men got 18% to 28% fewer callbacks than white men [@braun-2026, p. 1].

## Is the bias spread evenly across employers?

No. In the largest US test, a small group of employers caused much of the bias.

The fifth of firms with the widest gaps caused nearly half of the replies lost by applicants with names read as Black. The authors could single out 23 of the 108 firms as discriminating against those applicants [@kline-2022, p. 2]. In 2024 the same authors published grades for 97 of the employers, by name [@kline-2024].

For you, this is the reason to apply widely. When a fifth of firms cause nearly half of the loss, the other firms cause less of it each [@kline-2022, p. 2]. Each added application is another chance to reach one of them. No field test we found has measured applying widely as a tactic, so this advice is our reading of the finding.

## Does "whitening" a resume get more callbacks?

In one small US test, yes. The change has a cost, and part of the method was not honest. This article does not recommend it or argue against it; the choice is yours.

A 2016 study sent 1,600 made-up applications to entry-level jobs in 16 US metro areas. Some resumes had the first name changed, some had clues in the experience removed, and some had both. Here are the results for the Black applicants, 200 applications per version.

```bars
Callbacks for Black applicants by resume version (Small study, 800 made-up applications) [@kang-2016, pp. 28-33]
Version | Callbacks
Nothing changed | 10%
First name changed | 13%
Experience changed | 18%
Both changed | 25.5%
```

The name change alone was not a clear gain; the gap could be chance. For Asian applicants, callbacks rose from 11.5% with nothing changed to 21% with both changed. Half of the job ads said the employer valued diversity. The gaps were about as large at those employers [@kang-2016, pp. 28-33].

The test changed experience by renaming organizations so that words naming a group were gone. On a real resume, a renamed organization is a false fact. The honest version is to leave an item off or describe it in general words.

The study also interviewed 59 Black and Asian university students. Of those students, 36% said they had changed their own resumes this way. The authors warn the practice may take a toll, and one student spoke of having to "squash" parts of an identity [@kang-2016, pp. 7-9, 35-36].

## Do anonymous resumes fix it?

Not reliably. Two trials found that hiding names made things worse for the groups it was meant to help.

France tested anonymous resumes through its public employment service from November 2009 to September 2010. Firms chose whether to join. With names shown, 9.3% of minority candidates got an interview. With names hidden, 4.7% did. The gap between minority and majority candidates widened by about 10.7 points. The firms that joined already interviewed minority candidates about as often as others, so those firms had the least bias to remove [@behaghel-2015, pp. 13-14, 19].

Australia ran a lab trial in 2017. More than 2,100 public servants from 14 agencies each shortlisted made-up candidates for a senior job. With names shown, reviewers were 2.9% more likely to shortlist women and 3.2% less likely to shortlist men. Most minority candidates also did better with names shown, though only one of those differences was clear. The report says reviewers knew they were in a study, and real hiring may differ [@hiscox-2017].

Removing a question can backfire in the same way. New Jersey and New York City stopped employers asking about criminal records on application forms. Before the change, white applicants got 7% more callbacks than Black applicants at employers that asked. After it, the gap was 43% [@agan-starr-2018]. The authors think employers guessed at records from race once the form could not ask. We read that study's published summary only.

## Does age or a break in work count against you?

Yes to both, with limits. Each has its own article here.

Older applicants were about 42% less likely to get a positive reply across 17 field tests [@lippens-2023]. US tests found no drop at 40 [@farber-2017; @neumark-2019]. The ages, the dates that show age and the state rules are in [Age discrimination in hiring](age-bias-hiring.md).

Breaks of 13 months or more cut employer replies by about a fifth or more in a 2026 review of 16 field tests [@dhert-2026]. A health reason did better than no reason in one US test, and a childcare reason changed nothing in a UK test [@namingit-2021; @kristal-2023]. The details are in [Does an employment gap on your resume hurt?](employment-gaps.md)

## What about a criminal record?

A record cuts callbacks sharply, and the law limits what employers may ask. This is general information, not legal advice.

Employers that asked about records were 63% more likely to call back applicants who had none [@agan-starr-2018].

As of October 2026, federal contractors may not ask about criminal history for a job under a federal contract before a conditional offer. The statute has exceptions [@usc-41-4714]. By one count from October 2021, 15 states and 22 cities and counties stopped private employers asking on the application form [@nelp-2021].

Some states also limit what may be asked at all. As of October 2026, California bars employers from asking about an arrest that did not lead to a conviction. The same law covers a conviction that a court dismissed or sealed [@ca-labor-432-7].

A court certificate may help where a state offers one. A small Ohio test found that a certificate more than tripled the chance of an interview invitation or job offer. Certificate holders did nearly as well as applicants with no record [@leasure-2016]. We read that study's published summary only.

## Is AI resume screening biased?

In lab tests, often, and the direction changes. A 2026 preprint tested 14 AI models on 24,024 resume pairs each that differed only by name. Which group a model favored changed between models and versions [@gao-2026]. Another 2026 preprint found models picked up background clues from resumes with no name on them [@chen-xiao-2026]. Lab tests show what models do in a test, not what employers do. The tests are in [Is AI resume screening biased?](ai-resume-screening-bias.md) The rules on notice are in [AI hiring laws](ai-hiring-laws.md).

## What can employers do?

The studies here point to checked, shared rules for hiring. The evidence is thin.

- In the largest US test, firms that handled applicant contact centrally had smaller gaps. Federal contractors did too. Both are links, not proof of cause [@kline-2022, p. 2].
- In the 2026 preprint, gaps were narrower in jobs screened on things an employer can check, such as tests and certificates [@braun-2026, p. 1].
- Diversity wording in a job ad did not predict fairer screening in the 2016 test [@kang-2016, pp. 28-33].

## What we don't know

- Whether applying to more employers raises one person's chance of a fair reader. We found no field test of it. We searched the web and arXiv in October 2026.
- Whether leaving an item off works as well as the renamed organizations in the 2016 test. No field test we found compared them.
- Whether structured interviews cut bias in real hiring. They are often advised, but we did not open a study of them for this article.
- How many states limit record questions today. The count used here is from 2021 [@nelp-2021].
- Whether older tests still hold. The tests in the 2017 review ran in 2015 or earlier [@quillian-2017].

## What helps

- Apply widely. Bias was concentrated in a minority of large employers [@kline-2022]. That is our reading of the finding, not a tested tactic.
- Keep facts true. In a 2025 survey by a background-check company, more than three-quarters of businesses had found mismatches in applicants' details [@hireright-2025]. That company sells those checks.
- Your name and the groups on your page are your call. Initials or a name you go by are honest; a legal-name box needs your legal name. Never rename a group.
- With a record, read the exact question and answer only what it asks. Check your state's rules with free legal aid.
- Prepare for the interview as well as the resume. Bias after the callback was larger than bias before it [@quillian-2020].
